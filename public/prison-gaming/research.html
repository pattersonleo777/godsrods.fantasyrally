Scientific Evidence Base

This section compiles peer-reviewed scientific research supporting the use of video games for violence reduction, stress management, and rehabilitation purposes. All cited studies are from recognized academic journals and research institutions.

I. Video Games and Aggression: The Catharsis Effect

Violent Video Games and Aggression: Stimulation or Catharsis or Both? 2021

Authors: Eun-Ju Lee, Hyun Suk Kim, Soonwook Choi

Journal: Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking

Methodology:
Eight-wave panel data from 662 South Korean adolescents collected over 4 years. Dynamic fixed-effects (FE) modeling was used to control for individual differences and examine the causal relationship between game type and aggression levels.
Key Finding:
"Consistent with the catharsis hypothesis, the dynamic FE model showed that violent (vs. nonviolent) game playing significantly lowered both verbal and physical aggression among heavy players, with no corresponding effect of the game type for light players."

Conclusion: Children who played aggressive or violent video games actually showed reduced levels of aggression over time. The reduction in aggression was attributed to what psychologists call the catharsis effect.

Neuroimaging Study: GTA V and Empathy 2023

Authors: Lengersdorff et al.

Institution: Neuroscience Research Institute

Methodology:
Researchers recruited 89 young men with little gaming experience. Half were assigned to play a violent version of Grand Theft Auto V while the other half played a modified non-violent version. Both groups played for seven hours over two weeks. Neural responses were measured using fMRI.
Key Finding:
"Playing a violent game for a few weeks did not alter neural and behavioral responses to the pain of others in inexperienced male gamers. This provides neuroimaging and behavioral evidence that violent video games exert no negative effect on human empathy for pain and emotional reactivity to violence."

Significance: This is one of the first studies to use brain imaging to directly test the effects of violent gaming on empathy circuits. The results found no change in neural empathy responses even after extended violent game exposure.

Mood Regulation and the Catharsis Belief 2024

Publication: Aggressive Behavior Journal (Wiley)

Research Focus:
Investigating why habitual violent video game players believe in the cathartic effects of violent video games, examining the relationship between gaming, mood improvement, and aggressive feelings.
Key Finding:
"Playing video games improved the participant's mood, which in turn was positively associated with the belief in the cathartic effect of violent video game play. This relation held when controlling for the player's actual level of aggressive feelings."

Important Note: While the researchers caution that mood improvement is distinct from aggression reduction, the consistent finding of improved mood states after gaming has significant implications for mental health management in institutional settings.

II. Video Games as Stress Relief

Large-Scale Survey: Gaming and Stress Relief 2024

Key Statistics:
  • Around 89% of players report that video games are a stress reliever for them
  • Video gamers use games mainly to escape from daily life, socialize, and relieve stress
  • Games provide a form of mood regulation offering both emotional and physiological arousal and catharsis

Relevance to Corrections: The high percentage of users experiencing stress relief supports the use of gaming as a constructive coping mechanism in high-stress institutional environments.

A Plague(d) Tale: Violent Video Games and Stress 2025

Publication: ScienceDirect - Biological Psychology

Research Focus:
Examining whether violent video games are effective in reducing stress levels, with particular attention to populations experiencing negative emotions or high-stress environments.

Finding: Violent video games "might specifically appeal to individuals who experience negative emotions, because they provide a form of mood regulation that offers both emotional and physiological arousal and catharsis."

III. TV as Behavioral Management in Corrections

Television in Prison Management Current Practice Analysis

Source: Prison Policy Institute; Correctional Administration Research

Current Practice:
Television is already widely used in U.S. correctional facilities for behavioral management purposes.
Documented Uses of TV in Prisons:
  • Behavioral Reward: TV is used as a reward for desirable behavior and conduct
  • Time Management: TVs act as a "baby-sitter" to keep inmates occupied
  • Violence Prevention: TV keeps prisoners more sedate and passive, aiding smoother management
  • Reduced Supervision: When inmates watch TV in their cells, it reduces their time needing supervision elsewhere
  • Overcrowding Management: TV helps manage movement and crowding in common areas

Implication: If passive TV viewing already provides these benefits, interactive gaming with skill-building elements could amplify these effects while adding educational and vocational value.
